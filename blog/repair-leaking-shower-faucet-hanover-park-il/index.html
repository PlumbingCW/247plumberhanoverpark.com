How to repair a leaking shower faucet

Fixing the drip starts with knowing which valve is behind your handle. Compression, cartridge, ball, and ceramic disc valves all repair differently.

By the Hanover Park 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026

Call (630) 822-9290

Start by finding out which valve you have

A shower that drips after you turn it off is almost always a worn internal part. The part depends on the valve type, and each type has a different repair. Guessing leads to wasted trips to the hardware store.

Look at the handle first. Two separate handles for hot and cold usually mean compression or two-handle cartridge valves. A single handle usually means a cartridge, ball, or ceramic disc valve.

How to tell the four common shower valves apart
Valve typeHow it feels and looksUsual cause of a dripPart to replace
CompressionTwo handles, turn many times to closeWorn rubber washer or damaged seatWasher and seat
CartridgeSingle handle that slides or pivots, or two handles with a quarter turnWorn O-rings or a cracked cartridgeCartridge or O-rings
BallSingle lever that rotates over a rounded capWorn seats and springsSeat and spring kit
Ceramic discSingle lever on a wide cylindrical bodyCracked disc or worn sealsDisc cartridge

Pull the handle to confirm

Turn off the water first. Look for a shower stop on the valve, or close the main shutoff if there is none. Open the shower to drain the line and plug the tub drain so no screw falls in.

Pop off the cap on the handle and remove the screw or set screw. Slide the handle off, then remove the trim plate. The valve body is behind it, and its shape tells you the type.

Repair steps for a compression valve

  1. Unscrew the bonnet nut with an adjustable wrench.
  2. Lift out the stem and look at the rubber washer at its tip.
  3. Replace the washer and check the brass seat for scoring.
  4. If the seat is rough, smooth it with a seat dresser or replace it.
  5. Coat the threads with plumber's grease and reassemble.

Repair steps for a cartridge or ceramic disc valve

  1. Remove the retaining clip, nut, or screws holding the cartridge.
  2. Note the cartridge position, because most must go back facing the same way.
  3. Pull the cartridge straight out with pliers or a puller made for it.
  4. Take it to a supplier and match the brand, size, and stem splines exactly.
  5. Lightly grease the new O-rings, seat the cartridge, and secure the retainer.

Repair steps for a ball valve

A ball valve needs a repair kit with new inlet seats, springs, and a cam. Remove the cap, collar, and ball, then drop out the old seats with a small screwdriver. Press the new seats and springs in, set the ball, and tighten the cap. Match the kit to the brand stamped on the body.

If the valve body is pitted or corroded, no kit will seal it. At that point the whole valve needs to be replaced, which means opening the wall.

When to stop and call a plumber

  • The shutoff does not hold, so water keeps flowing.
  • The valve is pitted or the stem is frozen in place.
  • You see water stains on the ceiling or wall behind the shower.
  • The leak continues after you replace the cartridge.
  • The wall must be opened to reach the valve.

Stains behind the wall suggest the leak is not at the handle at all. A plumber can use leak detection to find the real source before tile is cut. For a straightforward drip, faucet repair often takes a single visit.

Tools and parts to gather first

You need a few basics before you start. A hex key set and a Phillips screwdriver handle most handles. Add an adjustable wrench, pliers, a small utility knife, plumber's grease, and a cloth to cover the drain.

Take the old washer, O-ring, or cartridge with you to the supplier. Matching by sight and by feel is far more reliable than matching by name. Brand marks are often stamped on the cartridge, and they save time.

Mistakes that cause a second leak

Overtightening the bonnet or retaining nut can crack the valve body. Forcing a cartridge in the wrong direction can score the walls. Reusing an old, flattened O-ring seals poorly even if it looks fine.

Another common mistake is forgetting to turn the water back on slowly. A sudden surge can blow a loose cartridge out of its seat. Open the stop a quarter at a time and watch the trim for beads of water.

Finish by running the shower hot and cold for a few minutes. Check the ceiling below and the wall around the trim for damp spots after an hour.

Why a small drip is worth fixing

A drip of a few drops a minute adds up over a month. It also leaves mineral stains on the tub and wears the seat further each day. Fixing it early usually means a washer or O-ring instead of a whole valve.

A constant drip into an old cast iron tub can stain the enamel. It also puts steady load on the drain and trap, which is rarely a problem but is easy to prevent. Replace the worn part as soon as you can shut the water off.

If you rent or share the house, tell the owner promptly. Hidden leaks behind a shower wall can rot framing before anyone sees a stain.

Keep the old cartridge or stem in a drawer labeled with the date. If the new part fails early, you have a reference for the supplier and a way to compare wear.

Photos of each step help when you put the valve back together. Snap one after every part comes off.
